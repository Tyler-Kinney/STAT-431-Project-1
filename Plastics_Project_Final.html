Plastics During COVID: A Comparative Analysis of Plastic Waste from 2019-2020

Author

Tyler Kinney, Greyson Ballesteros

The introduction of novel coronavirus became a defining moment in global history when it made its first public appearance in Wuhan, China. The epidemic spread like wildfire, and has altered the course of humanity to this day. As the virus took its toll, humans began exhausting its resources like never before; one of these resources, of course, was plastics. As students that survived through the peak of COVID-19, we were interested in how the usage of plastics changed from 2019 to 2020, a span of years that has become one of the most relevant turning points in our lives.

Plastic Usage Year to Year

Have the types of plastics that are being used changed significantly from 2019 to 2020?

Data Context

Code 
tuesdata <- tidytuesdayR::tt_load('2021-01-26')
plastics <- tuesdata$plastics

The data we used for this project comes from Break Free from Plastic (BFFP) courtesy of Sarah Sauve. The observational unit is one cleaning project. The data contains variables relating to types of plastic, where the plastic comes from, and country. The columns in the data have been assigned certain classes like “double” or “character”. The creators of the data set also cleaned the names of the columns using the janitor package. They also took two data sets from 2019 and 2020 and bound the rows together. The audit was completed in October of 2020, implying that the data was collected during that time frame. The data was collected as part of a bigger project to understand the sources of plastic in different cities.

Analysis

To start, we wanted to create a table that showed proportional differences in countries’ usage of different plastic types from 2019 to 2020. The plastics we investigated were hdpe (high density polyethylene), ldp (low density polyethylene), pet (polyester plastic), pp (polypropylene), ps (polystyrene), pvc (polyvinyl chloride), and o (other plastic).

Code 
plastic_types <- c("hdpe", "ldpe", "pet", "pp", "ps", "pvc", "o")

Prop_change <- function(type) {
  plastics |>
    filter(parent_company != "Grand Total") |>
    group_by(year) |>
    summarize(
      total_type = sum(across(type), na.rm = TRUE),
      total_all = sum(grand_total, na.rm = TRUE),
      .groups = "drop"
    ) |>
    mutate(prop = total_type / total_all) |>
    arrange(year) |>
    summarize(
      plastic_type = type,
      prop_2019 = prop[year == 2019],
      prop_2020 = prop[year == 2020],
      change = prop_2020 - prop_2019
    )
}

While our earlier analysis shows how plastic usage changed overall from 2019 to 2020, it doesn’t tell us whether these changes were consistent across countries. To explore this further, we look at how total plastic waste shifted at the country level. By comparing each country’s plastic waste before and during the pandemic, we can see whether the global trends we observed were widespread or driven by a smaller number of countries.

Code

Figure 2 shows us that most countries experienced relatively small changes in plastic waste between 2019 and 2020, as indicated by the clustering of points near the dashed line. However, a few countries most notably the Philippines and Switzerland stand out with substantially larger increases. This suggests that while global plastic waste patterns did shift during COVID-19, much of the change may be driven by a small number of outlier countries.

Plastic Usage by Income Level

How does the distribution of plastic waste change with the different income levels of countries?

Data Context

To answer this question, we analyze how plastic waste is distributed across income groups and how plastic composition differs within those groups. This requires incorporating GDP data from 2019 and 2020 to classify countries by income level.

Code 
# reading in csv, found here https://github.com/lukes/ISO-3166-Countries-with-Regional-Codes/blob/master/all/all.csv
GDP_2019_2020 <- read.csv(here("GDP_data.csv"), skip = 4) |>
  select(Country.Name, X2019, X2020) |>

    cols = c(`2019`, `2020`),

  filter(country != "EMPTY") |>
  group_by(country, year) |>
  summarize(total_plastic = sum(grand_total, na.rm = TRUE), .groups = "drop")

combined_data <- plastic_summary |>

Updated_Plastics <- plastics |>

Table 3 shows a clear shift in how plastic waste is distributed across income groups between 2019 and 2020. In 2019, middle-income countries dominated plastic waste generation, accounting for over 60% of the total, far exceeding all other groups. To confirm whether these differences are statistically meaningful, we conduct a chi-square test of independence.

Table 5 displays a chi-square test of independence that was conducted to examine whether the distribution of plastic waste types differs across income groups. The results were statistically significant (df = 24, χ² = 118,175.67, p-value < 0.001), indicating that plastic type and income group are not independent, meaning the composition of plastic waste varies across income levels.

Conclusion

Overall, our analysis shows that plastic usage patterns shifted during the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic, with notable changes in both the amount and composition of plastic waste. While global trends suggest increases in certain plastic types such as polypropylene, country-level analysis reveals that these changes were not uniform and were often driven by a small number of outlier countries.

Additionally, income level plays a meaningful role in shaping plastic waste patterns. Both the composition and distribution of plastic waste vary across income groups, though these differences are often driven by changes in the relative importance of common plastic types rather than entirely different materials.

Together, these findings highlight that global disruptions like COVID-19 influence not only how much plastic is produced, but also how plastic usage varies across countries and economic contexts.

## Conclusion

Overall, our analysis shows that plastic usage patterns shifted during the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic, with notable changes in both the amount and composition of plastic waste. While global trends suggest increases in certain plastic types such as polypropylene, country-level analysis reveals that these changes were not uniform and were often driven by a small number of outlier countries.

Additionally, income level plays a meaningful role in shaping plastic waste patterns. Both the composition and distribution of plastic waste vary across income groups, though these differences are often driven by changes in the relative importance of common plastic types rather than entirely different materials.

Together, these findings highlight that global disruptions like COVID-19 influence not only how much plastic is produced, but also how plastic usage varies across countries and economic contexts.
